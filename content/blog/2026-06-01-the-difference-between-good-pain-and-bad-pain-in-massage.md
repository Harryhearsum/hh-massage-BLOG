One of the most common things I hear in my treatment room is someone saying "I'm not sure if this should hurt." Usually they're lying on the table, I'm working into a tight muscle, and they genuinely can't tell if what they're feeling is good pain or a sign that something's wrong. It's a fair question, and honestly, it matters because pushing through the wrong kind of pain can actually set you back.

The truth is that massage, particularly sports massage, involves some discomfort. But there's a massive difference between the kind of "ouch, that's working" feeling and actual pain that means you need to stop. Learning to spot the difference is the quickest way to get better results without injuring yourself further.

What most people don't realise is that your nervous system is incredibly sensitive to context. The same sensation can feel either therapeutic or threatening depending on a few factors, and understanding those factors changes everything about how you approach treatment. When you come in for [deep tissue massage work](/treatments), part of my job is teaching you to recognise what's actually happening in your body.

Understanding your pain signals during massage isn't just about making the session more comfortable. It's about getting you to trust the process so you can relax enough to let the work actually happen. Tension and bracing work against massage, every single time. Whether you're coming to my clinic in Treeton, travelling from Sheffield, or across South Yorkshire, that principle stays the same.

The difference between good and bad pain often comes down to how your body is responding. Good pain during massage usually has a few characteristics. You can describe it. It's typically a dull ache, a pulling sensation, or that intense but manageable pressure feeling that makes you want to breathe into it. You're actually breathing, not holding your breath. And it stays localised. When I'm working on a tight calf, you feel it in the calf. Not shooting down into your foot, not up into your knee.

Good pain also eases as you relax into it. That's the key thing. When I'm doing sports massage and working into a stubborn knot, the intensity doesn't stay at ten out of ten. Your nervous system gradually realises the pressure is controlled and safe, so your muscles settle and the sensation becomes more manageable. You might even feel some release happening during the session, or notice movement improving immediately afterward. That's how you know something productive is happening.

Bad pain is different. It's sharp, it's electric, it travels, or it doesn't feel like it's in the muscle at all. Sometimes clients describe it as a pinching sensation or a nerve-type feeling that makes them want to move away from the pressure. Bad pain doesn't ease when you relax. In fact, it often gets worse. It might make you hold your breath or tense up involuntarily. Your body's alarm bells are going off for a reason, and when that happens, you need to speak up immediately.

This is where most people go wrong. They tough it out thinking they need to endure deep tissue pain to get results. Then they go home, things feel worse the next day, and they think massage isn't for them. The problem is they let someone go too hard, too fast, in the wrong direction, and now their nervous system is protective and inflamed. I see this a lot with clients who've had massage before and had a negative experience.

Someone comes in from Rotherham or Sheffield saying "I had sports massage once and I was sore for a week." That's not how it's meant to work. Yes, you might have some soreness the next day, that's normal. But it should be a mild muscle soreness like after gentle exercise, not actual pain. There's a clear difference, and if you're not sure which you had, it probably wasn't right.

The intensity and speed of work matters enormously. I might be applying significant pressure, but if I'm working slowly and respectfully with your nervous system, it'll feel different than someone jabbing their thumbs in frantically. Controlled, deep pressure that lets your body adapt is therapeutic. Sudden, aggressive work that doesn't give your tissues time to respond is just trauma, and your body will guard against it.

Communication is absolutely critical here. This is why I always ask clients to give me feedback during treatment. If something doesn't feel right, you need to say so. I'm not offended. I'd much rather adjust my technique and get the session working properly than have you white-knuckling through something that's making things worse. That's just basic sense.

One thing I tell most of my clients is that your comfort level actually affects how well the treatment works. When you're tense and defensive, your muscles are contracted and harder to access. When you're relaxed and trusting, I can get deeper with less force, and the results are better. Speaking up when something's wrong isn't weak, it's smart.

If you're having sharp pain, shooting sensations, nerve-type feelings, or pain that doesn't ease with relaxation, that's a signal to pause. If you're having that intense but manageable dull ache that eases slightly as you breathe, that's usually productive. If you're not sure, ask. It's my job to know what I'm doing and to listen to what your body is telling you.

Your body is constantly communicating during massage. A good therapist pays attention to how you're responding, not just physically but through your breath and tension levels. I can tell when someone's nervous or bracing because their muscles stay tight. I can also tell when they're starting to release because the quality of the tissue changes under my hands.
