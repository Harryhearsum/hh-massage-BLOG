Winter training feels like running against the elements. The mornings are dark, the air is cold, and your muscles feel tight before you even start moving. I see a lot of people come through the clinic in January and February with injuries that could have been prevented with a bit of planning. The cold doesn't just make training uncomfortable, it genuinely changes how your body behaves and where the injury risk sits.

When the temperature drops, everything tightens up. Your muscles become less elastic, your connective tissues stiffen, and blood flow becomes less efficient in delivering oxygen and nutrients to the areas that need them most. That's not an excuse to stop training, but it does mean you need to be smarter about how you approach it. I work with runners, gym goers, and weekend warriors across Rotherham and Sheffield, and the pattern is always the same in winter, if you're not warming up properly and managing the extra load on your body, you're setting yourself up for problems.

The biggest mistake I see is people rushing straight into their normal workout intensity without giving their body time to adapt to the cold. You wouldn't jump into an unheated pool without easing in, yet that's exactly what happens when someone steps outside into freezing air and immediately tries to hit their usual pace or lift their usual weight. Your nervous system needs time to register what's happening. Your muscles need time to increase their temperature and improve their elasticity. A proper warm-up in winter isn't optional, it's the difference between staying healthy and spending six weeks dealing with a strained hamstring or a rotator cuff issue.

What I tell most of my clients is that a winter warm-up should be longer and more gradual than what you might do in summer. Start indoors if you can. Get some movement in before you step outside. A few minutes of gentle activity, some dynamic stretching that takes your joints through their full range of motion, and gradually building intensity makes a real difference. By the time you step out into the cold, your body is already primed to handle it.

Layering properly also matters more than people think. You want to manage sweat and keep your core temperature stable without restricting movement. Tight layers that hold heat are good, but they need to let you move freely. I've seen people come in with shoulder issues that were partly caused by wearing heavy, restrictive winter gear that changed their movement pattern. It's a small thing, but over weeks of training it adds up.

Recovery is where winter training really gets demanding. The cold stresses your system, your body burns more calories just maintaining temperature, and if you're training hard, you're creating a bigger recovery demand than you might in summer. This is where [sports massage therapy](/treatments) becomes genuinely useful rather than just nice to have. Regular massage helps manage the tightness that builds up, keeps tissues mobile and healthy, and gives your nervous system a chance to shift into recovery mode. I'm not saying you need it every week, but if you're doing serious winter training, getting a massage every three to four weeks will sort out problems before they become injuries.

Sleep is non-negotiable. Winter naturally disrupts circadian rhythms because of the darkness, and if you're not protecting your sleep quality, your recovery suffers and your injury risk climbs. Keep your bedroom cool and dark, avoid screens before bed, and treat sleep like part of your training plan rather than something that happens if you have time.

Nutrition also matters differently in winter. Your body needs more energy to stay warm and train hard. If you're under-eating, you're not recovering properly, your tissues don't repair well, and nothing feels right in the gym or on the road. Make sure you're getting enough protein, enough calories, and staying hydrated even though you might not feel as thirsty in the cold.

The other thing that helps is variation in your training. This isn't unique to winter, but in the colder months people often get locked into one type of training because conditions are tough. If you're a runner, the icy paths mean you might do all your training on a treadmill. If you're a gym lifter, the weather doesn't change much for you, but the psychological pressure of training indoors all the time can push people to increase intensity to stay motivated. Mixing things up, cross-training, or dropping intensity on some sessions gives your body a chance to recover while still maintaining fitness.

If you do pick up an injury during winter training, the worst thing you can do is ignore it and hope it goes away. Small niggles that seem fine in January can become real problems by March if you're not addressing them. Getting a sports massage early, when something first starts to feel off, often sorts it out before it becomes a proper injury. I work with clients across South Yorkshire who wait weeks before getting treatment, and by then what could have been fixed in one or two sessions takes much longer.

The reality is that winter training is harder on your body. That doesn't mean you can't do it successfully, it just means you need to be intentional about warmth, recovery, preparation, and listening to what your body is telling you.
