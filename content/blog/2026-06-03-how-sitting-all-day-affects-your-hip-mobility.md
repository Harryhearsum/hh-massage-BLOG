If you spend eight hours a day sitting at a desk, your hips are slowly getting tighter. I see this constantly with clients who work in Sheffield and across South Yorkshire, and honestly, it's one of the most preventable problems I treat. The thing is, most people don't realize their hip mobility is shot until they try to do something simple like tie their shoelaces without bending their back, or they go to the gym and suddenly can't squat properly.

Sitting isn't inherently bad, but the way we do it is. When you're hunched over a keyboard, your hip flexors are in a shortened position for hours at a time. Your hip flexors are the muscles at the front of your hip that pull your knee up toward your chest. Over eight hours, they're not just sitting in that bent position, they're actively contracting against the chair and desk setup. By the time you've done that five days a week for months or years, those muscles have essentially forgotten how to lengthen properly.

What makes it worse is that most desk workers don't move much between sitting sessions. You shift from the office chair to the car seat to the sofa at home. Your hips stay in that flexed position the entire day, and by evening they're basically locked. This is where most people go wrong, they stretch once before bed and expect it to fix itself. A quick quad stretch for thirty seconds isn't going to undo eight hours of shortening.

The knock-on effects are genuinely surprising to people when I explain them. Tight hip flexors pull on your lower back, which is why so many desk workers complain about lower back pain that doesn't respond to normal stretching. Your glutes switch off because they're being stretched passively all day while you're sitting, which means they're not firing properly when you stand up or try to exercise. Your hip mobility gets restricted in multiple directions, not just in the direction you'd expect. I had a client from Rotherham come in last month who couldn't even sit cross-legged without significant discomfort, and it all traced back to years of desk work.

Another thing I notice is that tight hips affect your whole posture. When your hips are immobile, your spine has to compensate. You end up rounding your back more, your shoulders creep forward, and suddenly you've got neck tension and shoulder pain layered on top of the hip problem. It becomes this cascading pattern that's harder to fix than if you'd addressed the hips alone.

So what can you actually do about it? The first step is breaking up your sitting. This doesn't mean a thirty-minute yoga session after work, though that would help. It means getting up every hour and walking for five minutes. Stand while you take calls. If you've got a choice between the stairs and the lift, take the stairs. Genuinely basic stuff, but I see it make a huge difference with clients who actually commit to it.

Stretching helps, but you need to do it properly and consistently. Your hip flexors need static stretches held for at least sixty seconds, ideally twice a day. Pigeon pose, low lunges, or just kneeling on one knee with your hips pushed forward. The key is consistency over intensity. Twenty seconds of stretching occasionally won't cut it. What I tell most of my clients is that if you're serious about fixing this, you're looking at daily stretching plus movement breaks during the day.
