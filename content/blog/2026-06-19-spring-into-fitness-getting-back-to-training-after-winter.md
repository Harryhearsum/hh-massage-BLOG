Winter does something to your training momentum. Whether you've had a genuinely quiet few months or you've just felt heavier and slower, most people who walk through my door in spring are dealing with the same thing: they want to get back into it properly, but they're nervous about doing something wrong. The good news is that returning to training doesn't have to mean injury if you're smart about it.

The mistake I see most often is people jumping straight back to where they left off in autumn. They remember the weight they were lifting, the distance they were running, the intensity they could handle, and they think three months off hasn't changed anything. It has, though. Your nervous system has downregulated, your muscles have lost some tone, and your connective tissue needs waking up properly. This is where most people go wrong.

Your body is actually more resilient than you think, but it's also more sensitive to sudden changes than you'd expect. The winter break wasn't wasted time, even if it feels like it was. Your muscles and joints have been resting, which is good. The problem comes when you ask them to go from resting straight into work without a proper introduction. That's when minor tweaks become actual injuries that keep you sidelined for weeks.

What you're looking for is a gradual ramp up. If you were running regularly before, start with shorter runs at an easier pace than you remember being able to do. If you were lifting, drop the weight and focus on movement quality for the first couple of weeks. If you were doing a sport or activity that's seasonal, get back into it slowly even though the enthusiasm is there to dive straight in. I know that sounds boring, but I promise you it's the fastest way to get back to your previous level without stopping yourself with an injury.

The first two to three weeks back is about recalibrating. Your body needs to remember how to move properly under load again. You might feel muscles you'd forgotten about. Your joints might feel a bit sticky. That's normal. Where it becomes a problem is if you ignore those signals and push through, because that's usually when something gives way.

This is also where sports massage becomes really useful during the return to training phase. I see a lot of clients in spring who are getting back into running or gym work, and they're tight in places they weren't before. Calves, hamstrings, hip flexors, lower back, shoulders, neck. The winter posture creeps in, the movement patterns shift slightly, and suddenly when you try to load those tissues properly again, they're not quite ready. A good sports massage helps reset that. It gets the blood flowing back through muscles that have been under-used, it sorts out the movement restrictions, and it gives you actual feedback about which areas need extra attention during your training.

I'm based here in Rotherham, and this time of year I'm basically running an informal clinic for people getting back to Treeton's local gyms or heading back out onto the South Yorkshire running routes. The pattern is always the same. People come in stiff, we work through the restrictions, and suddenly they can move properly again. That makes a massive difference to how quickly they can progress with their training.

Something else that helps is paying attention to the movements you're doing in your daily life too. If you're working a desk job in Sheffield or Rotherham, you're probably spending most of your day in a fairly flexed position. That's working against you when you're trying to get back to full training. So spending even five minutes in the morning doing some basic mobility work, just getting your hips and shoulders moving properly, actually makes a difference to how your training sessions feel.

The temptation in spring is real, though. The weather's better, everyone around you is talking about their fitness goals, and you can feel the motivation building. Just remember that a cautious first month gets you back to proper training faster than jumping in too hard and having to take three weeks off with a strain.

If you're feeling tight, restricted, or you're noticing pain during your movements as you get back into it, that's worth sorting out sooner rather than later.
