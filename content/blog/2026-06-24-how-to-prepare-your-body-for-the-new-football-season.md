If you've had a few weeks off after the season ended, jumping straight back into competitive football without proper prep is a recipe for injury. I see it every summer with players coming in nursing hamstring tears or ankle problems that could've been prevented. Your body's adapted to having some downtime, and you can't just flip a switch and go back to full intensity. Pre season preparation isn't just about fitness, it's about getting your body physically ready to handle the demands of a forty-plus game season.

The problem a lot of footballers make is confusing match fitness with actual readiness. You might be able to run five kilometres without stopping, but that doesn't mean your muscles, joints, and connective tissues are primed for the explosive movements, direction changes, and contact that come with football. This is where a structured approach makes a real difference, and it's something I'm working on with quite a few players across Rotherham and Sheffield every summer.

Your muscles need time to remember what they're supposed to be doing. After a break, even a deserved one, your neuromuscular system loses some sharpness. This is why the first two to three weeks of pre season should focus on building a foundation rather than hitting peak intensity. Start with lower impact work like cycling, swimming, or tempo running. Get your aerobic base back first, then layer in the intensity. This gives your ligaments, tendons, and joints a chance to adapt gradually. If you jump straight into high-speed drills and plyometric work, you're asking for trouble.

Strength work is something I can't stress enough. Most footballers think about legs and core, which is fair enough, but what I tell most of my clients is that your upper body stability matters just as much when you're being challenged for the ball or trying to stay balanced during a tackle. A solid four-week block working on compound movements like squats, deadlifts, single-leg work, and loaded carries sets you up properly. Don't just do leg press machines in the gym. Get outside, work uneven surfaces, do loaded walks with dumbbells. Your stabiliser muscles need activation in ways that a seated machine can't provide.

Range of motion and mobility become critical once you start accelerating the football-specific work. This is where most players go wrong. They stretch for thirty seconds, call it a day, and then wonder why their hips feel stiff when they need explosive power. Mobility takes time and consistency. Work on your hip flexors, glutes, hamstrings, and thoracic spine every single day. Use foam rolling, active stretching, and movement prep drills. Spend time in loaded positions that you'll find yourself in during a match. A few minutes of quality mobility work before every session is better than an hour of static stretching once a week.

Your body's recovery systems need conditioning too. After time off, your nervous system takes a beating when you ramp intensity back up. This is where sports massage becomes genuinely useful rather than a luxury. I'm seeing players in my clinic across South Yorkshire who are keen to get fit but haven't considered how massage speeds up recovery and keeps tissue quality high. A good sports massage every seven to ten days during pre season helps with tissue quality, keeps your muscles supple, and gives you feedback about what's tight or needs attention before it becomes an injury. It's preventative work, not just something you do when something hurts.

Nutrition and hydration sound basic, but they shift dramatically once you're training seriously again. You can't just eat what you want during the off season and expect to function at your best come August. Get back to consistent protein intake, sort out your carbohydrate timing around your training sessions, and start paying attention to how you're fuelling. Dehydration creeps up on you faster than you'd think when you're ramping back into football-specific work, especially if you're training through summer heat.

The mental side matters too. Your body performs better when your head's in it. Use the first couple of weeks to set intentions for the season. What do you want to achieve? How are you going to be a better version of yourself? This mindset feeds into your preparation and makes you more diligent about doing the boring stuff like mobility work and eating properly.

One thing that makes a real difference is getting a movement assessment before you start intense training. Having someone watch how you move, spot asymmetries or restrictions, and give you specific guidance on what your weak points are means you can build a pre season plan that actually addresses your individual needs rather than just following a generic football fitness programme. This is something I do with clients who want to get it right, and honestly it's the difference between a smooth pre season and spending six weeks managing niggling injuries.

So here's what a solid pre season looks like: weeks one and two are about rebuilding aerobic fitness and getting mobility dialled in with lighter strength work. Weeks three and four you layer in intensity, start football-specific movements, and keep the recovery protocols tight. By week five you're ready for pre season friendlies. Throughout, you're doing consistent mobility work, eating well, staying hydrated, and getting some hands-on work done to keep your tissues healthy.

If you're serious about having a strong season without injuries holding you back, starting this prep now matters.
