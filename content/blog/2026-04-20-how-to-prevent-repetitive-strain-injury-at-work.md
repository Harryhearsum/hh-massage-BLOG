If you're spending eight hours a day at a desk, your body is under constant, low-level stress that you probably don't even notice until something starts hurting. Repetitive strain injury doesn't announce itself with a bang. It creeps up gradually, usually starting as mild discomfort that you dismiss, then slowly becomes something that actually interferes with your work and your life outside it. I see this pattern constantly with clients coming through my clinic in Treeton, and the frustrating bit is that most of these injuries are completely preventable.

The reason RSI develops is actually quite simple. Your muscles, tendons, and nerves adapt to the positions you put them in repeatedly. If you're typing, mousing, and sitting in the same posture eight hours a day, those tissues start getting tighter, weaker in some places, and overloaded in others. Your body isn't designed for that kind of static repetition. Add in poor desk setup, stress, and not enough movement between tasks, and you've got a recipe for pain that can take months to sort out once it starts.

What I tell most of my desk worker clients is that prevention is genuinely easier than recovery. Once you've got an actual repetitive strain injury, you're looking at changing your habits, getting treatment, and being quite careful for weeks. The stuff I'm going to cover here takes minimal effort and can save you a lot of hassle.

Your workstation setup matters more than you think

Most people don't have a clue whether their desk is actually set up properly. You know the basics: monitor at eye level, chair supporting your lower back, feet flat on the floor. But there's more to it than that. Your keyboard and mouse need to be positioned so your elbows sit at roughly 90 degrees and your wrists stay neutral. This means your forearms should be parallel to the ground, not angled up or down. If your mouse is too far away or too high, you're constantly reaching and rotating through your shoulder and wrist. Over months, that adds up.

The position of your monitor matters too. If you're constantly looking down or to one side, you're putting sustained tension through your neck and upper back. Your eyes should be level with the top third of the screen. If you're using a laptop, get a separate keyboard and mouse, then prop the laptop up. I know it looks a bit clunky, but it's the difference between maintaining good posture and slowly wrecking your neck and shoulders.

One thing I see with a lot of workers in Sheffield and across South Yorkshire is that they'll sort out their desk at home, but their office setup is different. If you're moving between locations, take five minutes to adjust your chair and monitor at each one. That consistency really does matter.

Movement is your best insurance policy

This is where most people go wrong. They think prevention means sitting perfectly still in perfect posture. Actually, it's the opposite. Your tissues don't want you to stay in one position for hours on end, no matter how good your posture is. Movement keeps things mobile, keeps blood flowing, and prevents tissues from getting locked into repetitive patterns.

Set a timer to move every 45 minutes or so. It doesn't need to be a full workout. Get up, walk to the kitchen, stretch your arms overhead, roll your shoulders, shake out your hands. Do some gentle neck rotations. The point is to break up the static posture and give different muscles a turn. I'm not talking about a five-minute break every two hours. That's still too long. Frequent, brief breaks work much better than occasional long ones.

Stretching at your desk helps, but it's not enough on its own. You need actual movement and position changes. If you can do some light strength work as well, even better. Things like face pulls, scapular rows, or band pull-aparts help keep your upper back strong and balanced against all that forward positioning from typing and mousing.

What to do with your hands and arms

Your forearms and hands are under constant load when you're typing and using a mouse. That load goes through your wrists, elbows, and up into your shoulders. One of the simplest things you can do is be conscious of tension. A lot of desk workers grip their mouse way too hard and keep their shoulders braced up toward their ears without realizing it. That constant low-level tension is genuinely damaging over time.

Try to keep your grip relaxed. Your hand should be loose on the mouse, not clenched. Your shoulders should sit in a neutral position, not hiked up. When you're typing, your wrists should stay fairly straight, not bent back or down. If you notice you're getting tense, take 30 seconds and consciously relax. Shake out your hands. Drop your shoulders down. This kind of self-awareness is free and it works.

Stress and RSI are connected

There's something people often miss about repetitive strain injury. It's not just about the physical repetition. Stress plays a real role. When you're stressed or anxious, your muscles stay more tense. Your shoulders hunch up. Your grip tightens. You're less likely to take breaks or move around. Stress also slows down your recovery and makes minor aches worse.

If you can manage your stress even a little bit, your body will be more resilient to repetitive work. That might be exercise, meditation, proper sleep, or just taking actual breaks during your day. Even getting outside for five minutes can help reset your nervous system.

Know when to get help

If you're already feeling the first signs of RSI, pain that comes and goes, tingling in your fingers, or stiffness that's worse in the mornings, don't ignore it. That's your body telling you something needs to change. Catching it early makes a huge difference. The longer you wait, the more entrenched the problem becomes and the longer it takes to fix.

Getting treatment early, combined with the prevention stuff I've mentioned, usually means you can sort things out quickly.
