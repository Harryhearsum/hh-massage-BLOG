Most people think their tight muscles are just tight muscles. They'll stretch for five minutes, hope it sorts itself out, and then wonder why the problem comes back. The thing is, a lot of what feels like muscle tightness isn't actually the muscle itself at all. It's the fascia, and that's where myofascial release comes in.

I see this constantly with clients coming in from Sheffield and across South Yorkshire. Someone's got shoulder pain that won't budge, or their legs feel heavy and restricted, and they've already tried everything. Once we start working on the fascia properly, things change fast. But I need to explain what's actually happening first, because understanding it changes how you think about your body.

Fascia is basically the connective tissue that wraps around every single structure in your body. Your muscles, your organs, your nerves, your bones, even individual muscle fibres, all of it's enclosed in layers of fascia. It's kind of like a three-dimensional web that holds everything in place and allows movement to happen smoothly. When you're moving well, that fascia is hydrated and elastic. It glides. Everything works.

But when you sit at a desk all day, or you run the same route week after week, or you keep repeating the same movement pattern at the gym, that fascia gets dehydrated and tight. It can develop restrictions and adhesions, which is basically where the layers of fascia get stuck to each other instead of sliding past each other. When that happens, movement becomes restricted and you often feel pain or stiffness that stretching alone won't fix. This is where myofascial release becomes genuinely useful.

So what's the difference between a regular massage and myofascial release? A traditional massage is great for relaxing muscles and improving circulation, but it's not always targeting the deeper fascial restrictions. When I do myofascial release, I'm working much more slowly and methodically through the layers of tissue. I'm applying sustained pressure to those restricted areas and waiting for the fascia to release. It's not about working hard or causing pain. It's actually quite the opposite. The pressure is firm but controlled, and you're working with your body's natural response to slowly ease those restrictions.

A lot of people ask me whether they should stretch more or get myofascial release. The honest answer is that you probably need both, but myofascial release often needs to come first. If you've got fascial restrictions, stretching might feel good in the moment but it won't fix the underlying problem. I had a runner in from Rotherham last month who'd been stretching his hamstring for months because it felt tight. Once we released the fascial restrictions through the back of his thigh and lower back, the tightness disappeared without any extra stretching.

The treatment itself is different from a deep tissue massage. You'll feel pressure, yes, but I'm not trying to cause pain. I'm applying sustained pressure to areas of restriction and then moving through the tissue slowly, waiting for it to release. Some areas respond quickly, other areas need a bit more time. You might feel what people describe as a release or a melting sensation as the fascia lets go. It's actually quite pleasant once you understand what's happening.

What conditions respond well to myofascial release? Runner's knee, lower back pain, shoulder impingement, plantar fasciitis, tennis elbow, and persistent stiffness in areas that don't seem to improve with stretching. I see it work brilliantly for desk workers who've developed restrictions through their neck and shoulders, and for people who've had old injuries that never quite felt right. The key is that these are situations where the problem isn't just muscle fatigue, it's fascial restriction.

Do you actually need it? That depends. If you're moving well, not in pain, and your flexibility is fine, then maybe you don't right now. But if you've got persistent tightness or pain, or if you're noticing that certain movements are getting restricted, then yes. Especially if you've tried stretching and foam rolling and it's not really moving the needle.
