Most people I see in clinic who've picked up a niggling injury did the same thing: they skipped the warm up. They rolled up to the gym or the track, got impatient, and went straight into their working sets. Then three weeks later they're messaging me with a tight shoulder or a dodgy knee that won't shift.

The truth is, warming up isn't just something you do because it feels good. It's genuinely one of the most protective things you can do before you train, and yet it's probably the most commonly rushed part of any session.

Let me explain what's actually happening in your body when you warm up properly, because once you understand it, you'll never skip it again.

When you start a training session cold, your muscles are literally stiff. The fluid inside your joints is thicker and slower to move. Your nervous system isn't firing on all cylinders. Your heart rate is sitting at resting levels and your blood isn't being directed to the working muscles yet. You're essentially asking your body to go from zero to intense without giving it any warning. That's when things go wrong.

A proper warm up gradually increases your core temperature, gets blood flowing to the muscles you're about to use, and tells your nervous system that you're about to do something demanding. This isn't some gentle stretching session. That's a separate thing entirely, and mixing them up is where most people go wrong.

What I tell most of my clients is that your warm up should be specific to what you're about to do. If you're going for a run, you don't need to spend ten minutes on a bike. If you're doing heavy squats, dynamic leg work makes sense. General cardio followed by movement that's relevant to your session is the formula that works.

The reason sports massage therapists and strength coaches bang on about this is that when you warm up properly, your muscles are more elastic and your joints have more stability. Your movement patterns are sharper. You actually perform better, which sounds obvious but it matters. I see this a lot with clients who come in having trained without warming up. They've compensated during their session, loaded the wrong muscles, and created tension patterns that take weeks to sort out.

Think about the runners I work with across Sheffield and Rotherham. The ones who crush their sessions without injury are the ones who spend a solid ten to fifteen minutes building into it. A bit of easy jogging, some dynamic leg swings, maybe some walking lunges. Then they go hard. Compare that to the person who does a two minute warm up and wonders why their IT band gets angry, or their hip flexors feel tight. The difference isn't luck.

Beyond just preventing injury, warming up properly means your nervous system is primed and ready to work. You'll recruit more muscle fibers during your session. You'll get better quality reps. Your strength output will be higher. For someone training in a South Yorkshire gym, whether you're in Rotherham, Sheffield, or anywhere else, this is the difference between a session that moves the needle and one that doesn't.

Here's what happens when you don't warm up enough: your muscles are tight, your joints aren't lubricated, and your nervous system hasn't gotten the signal that effort is coming. You then go into your heavy compounds or your sprints or your intense rounds, and something has to give. Usually it's a smaller stabilizer muscle that wasn't prepared, or you move through poor range because everything's stiff. That compensation pattern is what creates pain and dysfunction later.

I've worked with plenty of people who've had recurrent injuries that disappeared once they actually committed to a warm up routine. Not because the injury was particularly severe, but because they were doing it over and over by training cold and then loading aggressively. Add in some targeted [sports massage](/treatments) to address any existing tightness, and you've got yourself a proper foundation.

The other thing worth saying is that how long your warm up takes depends on how intense your session is going to be. A steady gym session might need ten minutes of prep. A heavy strength block might need fifteen or twenty. If you're doing max effort work, you need longer. The harder you're about to train, the more your body needs that gradual build up.

What I always say to clients is this: a good warm up doesn't cost you anything except time, but a bad or missing warm up costs you weeks of dealing with injuries that could have been prevented. You wouldn't drive a cold engine hard. Don't do it with your body either.

If you're training regularly and you're not spending real time on a proper warm up, it's worth looking at why. Impatience is normal, but you're essentially gambling. The extra ten minutes you add to the front of your session is probably the most valuable ten minutes of the whole thing.
